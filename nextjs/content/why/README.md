This is a course on making media in the age of AI. A few of you may go on to make YouTube videos or design video games. Most of you will make other things: a presentation to your company's board, a report or a tool for your lab, a multimodal essay. All term we have been asking what you can borrow from each mode of media production for that work.

Games are the most complex mode we have tried, and the one fewest of you will make for a living (not many of you will ship a AAA game). For borrowing, they may be the most important yet.

## Media that answers back

In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates complains that writing can't answer you:

> I cannot help feeling, Phaedrus, that writing is unfortunately like painting; for the creations of the painter have the attitude of life, and yet if you ask them a question they preserve a solemn silence. And the same may be said of speeches. You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer.

Plato, *Phaedrus* 275d, translated by Benjamin Jowett ([MIT Internet Classics Archive](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedrus.html)).

A game does answer back. It responds to every move you make, and it can teach you while it does. If we want to get past our current ways of communicating, writing above all, and especially the media people take in on their own time, we will want to give the reader or viewer more than one unvarying answer. We might start thinking of them as the player.

## What you can borrow

You can see the beginnings of this in interactive dashboards. Compare embedding one stock chart in a report on a company with embedding a table of stock charts, or of inflation data, that the reader can explore. The first shows one key view; the second lets the reader ask the data a question.

Last week we worked on levelling up live performance. This week asks the reverse: how do you make an asynchronous piece of media, on the web or anywhere else, feel alive? A map you can explore, a screen that keeps many moving parts in view, a mechanic that makes a presentation or a lab report respond to the person using it: these all come from games.

## This week: *Grand Theft Auto VI*

*Grand Theft Auto VI* comes out on **Thursday, November 19**. The series that was once the leading example in arguments about violent video games is now reviewed as art. *The New York Times* made it a Critic's Pick; its critic, Yussef Cole, wrote: "This is a game about love and relationships, their joy and heartache, and the work required to make them successful" (as quoted by [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/grand-theft-auto/grand-theft-auto-6-review-roundup-rockstars-magnum-opus-a-great-love-story-and-a-new-bar-for-open-worlds/)). It scores 99 on [Metacritic](https://www.metacritic.com/game/grand-theft-auto-vi/).

That is an old pattern. The novel, Dickens's monthly parts, early film, comic books and television were each dismissed as trash before they were taught and given prizes, and often what made them suspect was the new technology or mechanic that made them new.
