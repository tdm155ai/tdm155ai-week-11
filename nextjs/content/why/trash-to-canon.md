---
title: Trash to canon
description: When one era's trash became the next era's canon
---

New forms of media arrive cheap, popular, and suspect. A generation or two later the same forms are taught, collected, and given prizes. Often the thing that made them suspect was the technology or the mechanic that made them new: the cheap monthly part, the moving picture, the coin-operated game.

In 2011 the US Supreme Court summed up the pattern in a single paragraph, while ruling that video games are protected speech. Justice Scalia: "In the 1800's, dime novels depicting crime and 'penny dreadfuls' (named for their price and content) were blamed in some quarters for juvenile delinquency. When motion pictures came along, they became the villains instead. ... Radio dramas were next, and then came comic books. ... And, of course, after comic books came television and music lyrics." ([*Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Assn.*, 2011](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/08-1448.ZO.html))

Here are eight cases. As you read, ask which of the things made this year will be on this list in fifty years.

## 1. The novel

- **The form:** the long prose novel, read privately.
- **Then:** novels were something you apologized for reading. Jane Austen mocks the apology, and answers it, in *Northanger Abbey* (1817):

> "Oh! it is only a novel!" replies the young lady; while she lays down her book with affected indifference, or momentary shame.—"It is only Cecilia, or Camilla, or Belinda;" or, in short, only some work in which the greatest powers of the mind are displayed, in which the most thorough knowledge of human nature, the happiest delineation of its varieties, the liveliest effusions of wit and humour are conveyed to the world in the best chosen language.

- **Source:** [*Northanger Abbey*, chapter 5 (Wikisource)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Northanger_Abbey/Chapter_5)

## 2. Dickens and the monthly part

- **The form and the mechanic:** the serial. *The Pickwick Papers* came out in "flimsy shilling parts, issued in green wrappers once each month from April 1836 to November 1837," and its success "was unprecedented in the history of literature" (Robert L. Patten).
- **Not the first:** Patten quotes R. M. Wiles: "The piecemeal publishing of books ... was well established a hundred years before Dickens put pen to paper." Pierce Egan's *Life in London* had come out "in monthly shilling instalments" in 1821. Dickens did not invent the serial; he made it the most popular form of its day.
- **The numbers:** sales reportedly climbed from about 400 copies of the first part to about 40,000 by the end (unverified).
- **The cliffhanger is the mechanic:** a story built to make you buy next month's part.
- **Sources:** [Patten, "Pickwick Papers and the Development of Serial Fiction" (Victorian Web)](https://victorianweb.org/victorian/authors/dickens/pickwick/patten.html); [*Life in London* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_in_London_(novel))

## 3. The penny dreadful

- **The form:** cheap weekly fiction of crime and horror, a penny a part, for working-class and young readers.
- **Then:** "blamed in some quarters for juvenile delinquency" (the Supreme Court in 2011, looking back).
- **Now:** some of its characters are still with us: Sweeney Todd is usually traced to a penny serial, *The String of Pearls* (1846–47) (unverified).
- **Source:** [*Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Assn.* (2011)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/08-1448.ZO.html)

## 4. Early cinema

- **The technology:** the moving picture, shown for a few cents to anyone who walked in.
- **Then:** in 1915 the Supreme Court ruled that films could be censored because "the exhibition of moving pictures is a business, pure and simple, originated and conducted for profit, like other spectacles, not to be regarded ... as part of the press of the country, or as organs of public opinion."
- **Then, 37 years later:** "It cannot be doubted that motion pictures are a significant medium for the communication of ideas." (1952, overturning the 1915 ruling)
- **Sources:** [*Mutual Film Corp. v. Industrial Commission of Ohio* (1915)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/236/230); [*Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson* (1952)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/343/495)

## 5. Hitchcock and the coming of sound

- **The technology:** synchronized sound. Hitchcock's *Blackmail* (1929) "began production as a silent film"; the studio then had him shoot part of it with sound, and it "is frequently cited as the first British sound feature film." A silent version was released too, for cinemas not yet wired for sound. Hitchcock had been directing features for only about four years.
- **Then:** popular, and for a long time treated as a popular entertainer. *Vertigo* (1958) got mixed reviews; *The New Yorker*'s John McCarten wrote that Hitchcock had "never before indulged in such farfetched nonsense." *(As quoted by Wikipedia.)*
- **Now:** in 2012, 846 critics, curators, academics, and distributors voted in *Sight and Sound*'s poll of the greatest films, and "*Vertigo* (1958) had ousted *Citizen Kane* (1941) from its half-century reign at number one."
- **Sources:** [*Blackmail* (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackmail_(1929_film)); [*Vertigo*, reception (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertigo_(film)); [BFI, "The unveiling of the Greatest Films of All Time" (2012)](https://bfi.org.uk/features/unveiling-greatest-films-all-time)

## 6. Comic books

- **The form:** the cheap color comic book, sold on newsstands to children.
- **Then:** in April 1954 a Senate subcommittee held hearings "to determine whether comic books were corrupting the nation's youth." The psychiatrist Fredric Wertham testified: "Well, I hate to say that, Senator, but I think Hitler was a beginner compared to the comic-book industry. They get the children much younger." The industry answered with its own censor, the Comics Code.
- **Now:** Art Spiegelman's *Maus* received a special Pulitzer Prize citation in 1992.
- **Sources:** [Library of Congress, "The Senate Comic Book Hearings of 1954"](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/10/the-senate-comic-book-hearings-of-1954/); [Pulitzer Prizes, 1992](https://www.pulitzer.org/prize-winners-by-year/1992)

## 7. Television

- **The technology:** broadcast television in every living room.
- **Then:** in 1961 the new chairman of the FCC, Newton Minow, told the broadcasters to watch their own channel for a whole day: "I can assure you that what you will observe is a vast wasteland." He listed "game shows, formula comedies about totally unbelievable families, blood and thunder, mayhem, violence, sadism, murder ..."
- **Now:** long-form TV drama is reviewed like the novel and taught in universities (unverified).
- **Source:** ["Television and the Public Interest" (Wikipedia, quoting the speech)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_and_the_Public_Interest)

## 8. Video games

- **The technology and the mechanic:** the interactive screen. Unlike every form above, a game answers back.
- **Then:** in 2010 the film critic Roger Ebert declared that "Video games can never be art." *(As quoted by Reason in 2013.)*
- **Then, a year later:** the Supreme Court: "Like the protected books, plays, and movies that preceded them, video games communicate ideas—and even social messages—through many familiar literary devices (such as characters, dialogue, plot, and music) and through features distinctive to the medium (such as the player's interaction with the virtual world)."
- **Then, the year after that:** the Museum of Modern Art acquired 14 games, from *Pac-Man* and *Tetris* to *Portal*. Its curator, Paola Antonelli: "Are video games art? They sure are, but they are also design."
- **This week:** *Grand Theft Auto VI* comes out on November 19, 2026. *The New York Times*'s review (Yussef Cole) is a Critic's Pick: "This is a game about love and relationships, their joy and heartache, and the work required to make them successful." PC Gamer: "Grand Theft Auto was once the poster child for everything insidious, dangerous, and downright wrong about videogames," and now a UK cabinet minister calls it "something that we can be really proud of." More in [GTA VI this week](/why/gta-vi-this-week).
- **Sources:** [Reason, "The Time Roger Ebert Dismissed Video Games" (2013)](https://reason.com/2013/04/04/the-time-roger-ebert-dismissed-video-gam/); [*Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Assn.* (2011)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/08-1448.ZO.html); [Hyperallergic on MoMA's games (2012)](https://hyperallergic.com/one-up-moma-acquires-its-first-classic-video-games-as-art)

## Questions to take away

- In each case, what was new: the content, or the technology and the way people paid for and used it?
- Who did the dismissing, and who did the rescuing? (Look at who is speaking: senators, a judge, a critic, a museum.)
- What is being dismissed right now, and what would it take for it to land on this list?
