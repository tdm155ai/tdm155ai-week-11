This week's goal is to explore making a mechanic, with Claude. Choose one of two:

1. **Make a simple game that really works.** Small, finished, and playable by someone who isn't you. Forking one of [this week's games](/games), Wordle or Semantle, is a fine place to start.
2. **Design one game mechanic, or a game-like mechanic.** Either:
   - **a study toward a bigger game** you might make someday, one piece of it done well; or
   - **something inspired by games, put somewhere you actually need to make things**: a presentation, a lab report, a research tool, a report for a board, an essay.

## What a mechanic outside a game can look like

- **A really great map.** Maps and orchestration screens are clearly valuable for managing complex amounts of data: one place where you can see everything that's going on and move around in it.
- **A research tool** that lets you play with your sources rather than just list them.
- **An interactive chart instead of a fixed one.** Think of the difference between embedding one view of a stock chart in a report on a company and embedding a table of stock charts, or of inflation data, that the reader can explore.
- **Something that makes a piece of media feel alive** for the person who opens it later, when you aren't there to present it.

Writing doesn't answer you back. Games do. The question for this practice is what your work would look like if it did.

## Ways in

- **Start from a game you love.** Ask Claude to unpack the key mechanics of a game you play a lot, then pick one and rebuild it, or move it somewhere new.
- **Start from a mechanic.** Browse [the mechanics](/elements/mechanics) and pick one that could do something for your own work.
- **Start from a technology.** Whenever a new technology arrives, people make games out of it. What could you make out of a model, a camera, a microphone, or a map?

A prompt to begin with, in Claude Code:

```text
I want to build one game mechanic: [the mechanic, or the game it comes from].
I'd like to use it for [a game / a presentation / a lab report / a research tool / ...].
Before writing code, explain how the mechanic works in the original, what makes it
satisfying, and the smallest version of it I could build today as a web page.
```

## What to hand in

As with every practice: share it in the course Slack, add it to your GitHub repository for the course, and record a short video of yourself talking through what you made and what you learned. Keep it to about an hour of work (see the syllabus); if you'd rather propose a practice of your own of the same size or bigger, you can.

Whatever you make here can grow into [your final project](/final-project-and-portfolio).
